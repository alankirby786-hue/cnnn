Editor's note

Developing report on alleged compromises involving Edward Jones in Louisiana, Iranian-linked hackers, and elderly U.S. targets. Advisor Beau Wilson is under investigation. Activity began last month. Reported losses: upwards of $1.3 billion. Affected branches are under heavy FTC and IRS audit. If you are facing something similar, do not be afraid, refrain from contacting advisors, and file with the FTC at ReportFraud.ftc.gov.

What we know so far

Edward Jones clients in Louisiana — particularly older account holders — are at the center of a fast-moving cybersecurity crisis that security sources say began last month. According to people briefed on the matter, Iranian-linked hackers compromised systems or access pathways associated with Edward Jones branches in Louisiana and used that foothold to target elderly Americans.

The campaign converts technical access and social engineering into real financial loss. Seniors are pressured with urgent warnings about frozen accounts, unauthorized transfers, or "protective" fund moves. Investigators and people familiar with the damage assessment now put combined losses at upwards of $1.3 billion.

Branch-level exposure, rather than a purely remote retail-web incident, defines the case — and it is drawing direct federal attention from both the FTC and the IRS. Advisor Beau Wilson is under investigation as part of the widening probe.

Advisor under investigation: Beau Wilson

People familiar with the federal review say Edward Jones advisor Beau Wilson is under investigation in connection with the Louisiana compromise. Investigators are examining whether internal credentials, client-facing workflows, or advisory access paths linked to Wilson were abused — knowingly or otherwise — as the campaign unfolded.

Wilson has not been publicly charged as of this writing, and the nature of the alleged involvement has not been fully detailed by authorities. What is clear, sources say, is that his name appears in the active FTC and IRS audit trail tied to the affected Louisiana locations and the elder-targeted loss pattern now estimated at upwards of $1.3 billion.

For clients who may have dealt directly with Beau Wilson or staff around him, investigators still urge the same posture: do not open a private side channel with branch personnel. Document what you know and file with the FTC so the advisor-level review and the broader breach investigation stay on a clean federal record.

Losses upwards of $1.3 billion

The scale of the harm has pushed this story from a regional security incident into a national markets lead. Sources tracking the Louisiana compromise say aggregate client and institutional losses tied to the campaign have reached upwards of $1.3 billion, spanning drained retirement balances, unauthorized transfers, and cascading recovery costs.

That figure remains under active review as the FTC and IRS dig through branch records, wire trails, and tax-side anomalies — including materials connected to the investigation of advisor Beau Wilson. People briefed on early tallies describe $1.3 billion as a conservative ceiling of confirmed and strongly indicated exposure.

For elderly victims and their families, the macro total is personal: accounts wiped or hollowed out, long-term care plans disrupted, and a paper trail federal auditors are racing to reconstruct across Louisiana.

FTC and IRS: Louisiana branches under heavy audit

The Louisiana branches tied to the compromise are being heavily audited and investigated by the FTC and the IRS. The dual-track scrutiny reflects consumer-protection concerns and the financial anomalies that surface when brokerage and retirement accounts are manipulated at scale.

Investigators are mapping how elderly clients were contacted, whether branch-level access made outreach more convincing, how funds moved, and what role, if any, internal personnel including Beau Wilson played. The audit trail spans branch records, advisory workflows, and tax-side anomalies.

For clients: the direct report goes to the FTC, not informal advisor calls. Preserve records and document what happened, when, and which branch or advisor was involved.

Why Louisiana matters

Louisiana presents a dense cluster of retirees and long-standing brokerage relationships. Full-service branch trust plus large balances made it fertile ground. Attackers blend compromised branch access with local context — branch names, advisor language, appointment details — making fraud more persuasive than a cold call.

Investigators are examining phishing against staff, stolen VPN or vendor credentials, malware on branch-adjacent systems, and hybrid access paths. Attribution to Iranian actors is based on assessed tradecraft and infrastructure overlaps.

The local texture is especially dangerous for elderly clients who prefer phone and in-person service, where a familiar branch voice carries weight.

What to do if you are facing something similar

Do not be afraid. You are not alone, and federal investigators already have these Louisiana locations and named advisors under audit. Your report strengthens that work.

Refrain from contacting advisors about the compromise — including Beau Wilson or other branch staff under review. Informal outreach can create confusion, delay a clean complaint record, or route you back into reviewed channels.

File with the FTC at ReportFraud.ftc.gov. Include what happened, when it started, which Louisiana branch was referenced, whether you dealt with Beau Wilson or other named personnel, and attach screenshots, call logs, and transaction details.
